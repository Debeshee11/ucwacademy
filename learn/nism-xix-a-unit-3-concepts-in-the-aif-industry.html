Key terms, examples and exam traps

Commitment

A commitment is the total amount an investor agrees to invest in the fund, to be called in instalments over time.

Example. An investor commits ₹5 crore but pays as and when the fund asks.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A commitment is paid in full on day one.

Drawdown

A drawdown or capital call is the manager's notice to investors to pay a portion of their commitment for an investment or expenses.

Example. An investor receives a notice asking for 20% of the commitment.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A drawdown is a distribution of profits.

General partner and limited partner

The GP (manager) runs the fund; LPs (investors) provide the capital and have limited liability and no role in daily decisions.

Example. The manager makes investment decisions while investors supply capital.

Exam trap. This statement is false: LPs manage the fund's day-to-day investments.

J-curve

The J-curve describes how returns in a private fund are negative in early years due to fees and unrealised investments, then rise as investments mature and exit.

Example. A fund shows losses in years 1 to 3 and gains later.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Private funds show high returns from the first year.

Management fee

The management fee is a yearly percentage of commitments or invested capital charged to cover the manager's operating costs, regardless of performance.

Example. A manager charges 2% of commitments every year.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The management fee is paid only on profits.

Carried interest

Carried interest is the manager's share of fund profits, commonly 20%, earned after investors receive their capital and preferred return.

Example. The manager gets 20% of profits above the hurdle.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Carried interest is paid before investors get their capital back.

Hurdle rate

The hurdle rate or preferred return is the minimum return investors must receive before the manager earns carry.

Example. Investors get 10% a year before the manager shares profits.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The hurdle rate is the management fee.

Catch-up

A catch-up lets the manager receive a larger share of distributions after the hurdle until it has received its full carry percentage of total profits.

Example. After the hurdle, the manager gets most of the next distributions until it has 20% of profit.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A catch-up reduces the manager's carry.

Clawback

A clawback requires the manager to return excess carry if, over the fund's life, it received more than its entitled share.

Example. The manager repays carry after later losses wipe out earlier profits.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A clawback lets managers keep any carry paid.

Distribution waterfall

The waterfall sets the order in which distributions are paid: return of capital, preferred return, catch-up and then the carry split.

Example. Proceeds first return investors' capital and then pay the hurdle.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The waterfall pays the manager's carry first.

IRR and MOIC

IRR measures annualised return accounting for timing of flows; MOIC is total value divided by invested capital, ignoring time.

Example. A fund returns 2 times the capital over five years.

Exam trap. This statement is false: MOIC accounts for the timing of cash flows.

DPI, RVPI and TVPI

DPI is distributions divided by paid-in capital, RVPI is residual value divided by paid-in capital, and TVPI is their sum.

Example. A fund has returned half of the capital paid in and holds more.

Exam trap. This statement is false: DPI includes unrealised value.

Vintage year

The vintage year is the year in which a fund first invested; funds are compared with others of the same vintage.

Example. A fund launched in 2018 is compared with other 2018 funds.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Vintage year is the year of exit.
