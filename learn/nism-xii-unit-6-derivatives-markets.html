Key terms, examples and exam traps

Derivative

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from an underlying asset such as a share, index, currency or commodity.

Example. A contract whose value depends on the price of the Nifty 50.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A derivative has its own value, independent of any underlying.

Futures contract

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell an underlying at a fixed price on a future date, traded on an exchange with standard terms.

Example. A trader agrees today to buy an index lot at a set price at month-end.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A futures contract is an option with no obligation.

Option contract

An option gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell the underlying at a fixed price on or before expiry, in return for a premium.

Example. An investor pays a fee to keep the right to buy a share at ₹100.

Exam trap. This statement is false: An option obliges the buyer to complete the trade.

Call option

A call option gives its buyer the right to buy the underlying at the strike price.

Example. An investor expects prices to rise and buys the right to buy at ₹500.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A call option gives the right to sell.

Put option

A put option gives its buyer the right to sell the underlying at the strike price, and is used for protection or for gains when prices fall.

Example. An investor holding a share buys the right to sell it at ₹90.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A put option gives the right to buy.

Strike price

The strike price is the fixed price at which the option holder can buy or sell the underlying.

Example. An option allows the holder to buy at ₹2,500.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The strike price is the premium paid.

Premium

The premium is the price paid by the option buyer to the seller for the right granted.

Example. The buyer pays ₹20 per unit for an option.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The premium is returned to the buyer at expiry.

Hedging

Hedging uses derivatives to reduce the risk of adverse price moves in an existing position.

Example. A shareholder takes a position that offsets a possible fall in his holdings.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Hedging is aimed at maximising speculative profit.

Speculation

Speculation is taking a view on price direction and positions to profit from it, accepting the risk of loss.

Example. A trader buys index futures expecting a rise over the next week.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Speculation is risk-free.

Arbitrage

Arbitrage involves buying and selling related instruments at the same time to profit from price differences, with little risk.

Example. A trader buys a share in the cash market and sells its future at a higher price.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Arbitrage is a bet on market direction.

Lot size

The lot size is the fixed number of units in one derivatives contract, set by the exchange. Trading is in multiples of lots.

Example. An index future trades in minimum quantities of 75 units.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Investors can trade any number of units in a futures contract.

Expiry

Expiry is the date on which a derivatives contract ceases to exist and is settled.

Example. An options contract expires on a date fixed by the exchange.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Derivative contracts have no end date.

Margin in derivatives

Margin is the money a trader deposits with the broker as security for performing a futures or option-selling obligation.

Example. A futures trader deposits a fraction of the contract value as security.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Margin is the full contract value.

Mark-to-market

Mark-to-market is the daily adjustment of futures positions to the day's settlement price, with gains and losses settled in cash.

Example. A futures trader's account is credited or debited at the end of every day.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Mark-to-market happens only at expiry.
