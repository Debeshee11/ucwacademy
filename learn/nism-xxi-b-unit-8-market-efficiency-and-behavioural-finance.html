Key terms, examples and exam traps

Efficient market hypothesis

Under the efficient market hypothesis, prices reflect available information, making it hard to earn excess returns consistently.

Example. A manager finds it hard to beat the index after costs.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Prices never reflect any information.

Weak form

Weak form efficiency says current prices reflect all past price data, so technical analysis cannot earn excess returns.

Example. Chart patterns give no edge in a weak form market.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Weak form efficiency says insider information is already in the price.

Semi-strong form

Semi-strong efficiency says prices reflect all public information, so fundamental analysis of public data cannot earn excess returns.

Example. Published results are priced in quickly.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Semi-strong efficiency includes private information.

Strong form

Strong form efficiency says prices reflect all information, public and private, so even insiders cannot earn excess returns.

Example. Even insider information gives no advantage.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Strong form efficiency covers only past prices.

Random walk

A random walk means price changes are unpredictable because new information arrives randomly.

Example. Tomorrow's price change cannot be forecast from today's.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A random walk means prices follow a fixed pattern.

Market anomalies

Anomalies, such as size and value effects, are patterns that appear to contradict market efficiency.

Example. Small stocks have earned more than large over long periods.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Anomalies prove that markets are perfectly efficient.

Overconfidence

Overconfidence is overestimating one's own skill and information, which can lead to excess trading.

Example. An investor trades often, sure of her views.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Overconfidence leads to under-trading.

Loss aversion

Loss aversion is the tendency to feel losses more strongly than equal gains, which can lead to holding losers.

Example. An investor refuses to sell a falling stock.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Loss aversion makes investors sell losers quickly.

Herding

Herding is following the crowd rather than one's own analysis, which can feed bubbles.

Example. Investors rush into a hot sector.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Herding is independent analysis.

Anchoring

Anchoring is holding on to an initial reference, such as a purchase price, when making later decisions.

Example. An investor waits for the price to return to her cost.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Anchoring updates views fully on new data.
