Key terms, examples and exam traps

Ex-dividend date

The ex-dividend date is the date from which a share trades without the right to the declared dividend; buyers on or after it do not receive it.

Example. An investor buys on the ex-date and does not get the dividend.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Buyers on the ex-date receive the dividend.

Record date

The record date is the date on which a shareholder must be on the company's register to be eligible for a corporate benefit.

Example. Only holders on a specified date are entitled to the dividend.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The record date is the date the dividend is paid.

Bonus issue

A bonus issue gives existing shareholders extra shares free of cost in a ratio, capitalising reserves, with the price adjusting.

Example. A company gives 1 free share for every 2 held.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A bonus issue raises fresh funds for the company.

Stock split

A stock split reduces the face value and increases the number of shares in the same ratio, leaving market capitalisation unchanged.

Example. A ₹10 face value share splits into five shares of ₹2 each.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A stock split reduces the company's equity capital.

Rights issue

A rights issue offers existing shareholders the right to subscribe to new shares in proportion to their holding, usually at a discount.

Example. A company offers 1 new share for every 5 held at ₹150.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A rights issue is offered to new investors first.

Share buyback

A buyback is the repurchase of a company's own shares, reducing the share count and returning cash to shareholders, through tender offer or open market.

Example. A company buys back its shares at a premium to market price.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A buyback increases the number of shares.

Merger

A merger combines two companies into one, with the shareholders of the merged company receiving shares in the surviving company under a swap ratio.

Example. One company absorbs another and issues its shares to the other's holders.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A merger splits a company into two.

Demerger

A demerger splits a business into two or more entities, and shareholders receive shares in the new entity in a given ratio.

Example. A conglomerate separates its financial arm into a new listed company.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A demerger combines two companies.

Open offer

An open offer requires an acquirer who crosses the 25% trigger or acquires control to offer to buy shares from public shareholders under SEBI takeover rules.

Example. An acquirer who takes 26% must make an offer to other shareholders.

Exam trap. This statement is false: An open offer is made only to the promoters.

Delisting

Delisting is the removal of a company's securities from an exchange, either voluntarily or compulsorily.

Example. A company buys out public shareholders and its shares stop trading.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Delisting means a company issues new shares.

Dividend yield

Dividend yield is the annual dividend per share divided by the share price and shows income return.

Example. A share at ₹200 paying ₹6 yields 3%.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Dividend yield is profit per share.

Dividend payout ratio

The dividend payout ratio is the share of profit paid as dividends; the rest is retained for growth.

Example. A company pays ₹4 of ₹10 EPS, a 40% payout.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The payout ratio is dividend divided by price.
