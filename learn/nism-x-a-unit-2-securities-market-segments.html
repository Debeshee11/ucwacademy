Key terms, examples and exam traps

Equity market

The equity market is where shares are issued and traded, giving investors ownership and a claim on residual profits.

Example. An investor buys shares and shares in a company's future profit.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The equity market offers fixed returns.

Debt market

The debt market is where fixed-income securities such as bonds and treasury bills are issued and traded.

Example. An investor lends to a government in return for interest.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The debt market gives investors ownership of companies.

Primary and secondary markets

The primary market creates new securities and the secondary market trades existing ones, giving liquidity to investors.

Example. An IPO happens in the primary market; later trading happens in the secondary market.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Trading among investors takes place in the primary market.

Demat and trading accounts

A demat account holds securities electronically and a trading account is used to place orders through a broker.

Example. An investor needs both to buy shares on an exchange.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A demat account is used to place orders.

Settlement cycle

Equity cash market trades settle on T+1, meaning the next working day after the trade.

Example. A share bought on Monday is settled the next working day.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Equity trades take a week to settle.

Corporate bond market

Corporate bonds are debt securities issued by companies and carry credit risk, rated by rating agencies.

Example. A company raises money by issuing rated bonds.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Corporate bonds are free from credit risk.

Government securities market

G-secs are issued by the government to borrow, and carry virtually no credit risk, though prices move with interest rates.

Example. A government bond has no default risk but its price can fall when rates rise.

Exam trap. This statement is false: G-sec prices never change.

Money market instruments

Money market instruments mature within a year and include T-bills, CP, CD and repos.

Example. A company parks funds for 60 days in a short-term instrument.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Money market instruments mature after ten years.

Derivatives for advisers

Derivatives such as futures and options are used for hedging and speculation; advisers must understand leverage before recommending them.

Example. An adviser explains why a protective position may reduce a client's portfolio risk.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Derivatives remove market risk entirely.

Stock market indices

Indices such as the Sensex and Nifty 50 track the market and are benchmarks for portfolios and index funds.

Example. A portfolio manager compares returns with a broad market index.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Indices are fixed numbers that never change.

Short-term vs long-term capital gains

Gains on listed shares and equity funds are short-term if held up to 12 months and long-term if held longer, with different tax rates.

Example. An investor selling after 15 months realises a long-term gain.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The tax rate is the same regardless of the holding period.

Market risk of equity

Equity prices fluctuate with company performance and market sentiment, and capital can be lost, especially over short horizons.

Example. A client's equity fund falls 15% in a quarter.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Equities cannot lose value over short periods.

Settlement guarantee

The clearing corporation guarantees the settlement of exchange trades, reducing counterparty risk for investors.

Example. A trader need not worry that the other party will fail to deliver.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Exchange trades have no settlement guarantee.

Rating scales

Bond ratings range from the highest (AAA) to default (D), and investment grade is generally BBB- and above.

Example. A bond rated BB is considered below investment grade.

Exam trap. This statement is false: BBB is the highest rating.

Yield curve

The yield curve plots yields of bonds against their maturities; an upward-sloping curve is normal, where longer maturities yield more.

Example. Ten-year bonds yield more than one-year bonds.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The yield curve plots share prices against time.

Liquidity of securities

Liquidity varies by security: large-cap shares and G-secs trade easily while thinly traded bonds can be hard to sell.

Example. A client finds it difficult to sell a thinly traded bond.

Exam trap. This statement is false: All securities are equally liquid.
