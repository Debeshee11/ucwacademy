Key terms, examples and exam traps

Bull call spread

A bull call spread buys a lower strike call and sells a higher strike call, limiting both cost and profit.

Example. A trader expects a moderate rise in the dollar.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A bull call spread has unlimited profit.

Bear put spread

A bear put spread buys a higher strike put and sells a lower strike put to profit from a moderate fall.

Example. A trader expects a moderate fall in the dollar.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A bear put spread profits when prices rise.

Long straddle

A long straddle buys a call and a put at the same strike and expiry and profits from a large move in either direction.

Example. A trader expects a big move after an event but is unsure of direction.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A straddle profits when the market stays flat.

Short straddle

A short straddle sells a call and a put at the same strike, earning premium if the price stays near the strike, but with unlimited risk.

Example. A trader expects a quiet market.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A short straddle has limited risk.

Long strangle

A strangle buys an out-of-the-money call and put, cheaper than a straddle but requiring a bigger move to profit.

Example. A trader buys a 84 call and a 82 put.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A strangle is more expensive than a straddle.

Covered position

Combining an option with an underlying position, such as a short call against a long futures position, can enhance income but caps gains.

Example. A holder of futures writes a call for premium.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A covered position removes all risk.

Protective put

Buying a put against a long exposure sets a floor on losses while keeping the upside.

Example. An exporter holding dollars buys a put to protect against a fall.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A protective put caps the upside.

Maximum profit in a spread

In a bull call spread, maximum profit equals the difference in strikes less the net premium.

Example. Strikes 83 and 84 with net premium 0.30 give a maximum profit of 0.70.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Maximum profit equals the net premium.

Hedging a payable

An importer with a dollar payable can buy futures or a call option to protect against dollar appreciation.

Example. A firm buys a call to cap the cost of dollars.

Exam trap. This statement is false: An importer protects herself by selling dollar futures.

Hedging a receivable

An exporter with a dollar receivable can sell futures or buy a put to protect against dollar depreciation.

Example. A firm buys a put to set a floor on rupee proceeds.

Exam trap. This statement is false: An exporter protects herself by buying dollar futures.

Choosing between futures and options

Futures lock the rate but remove favourable moves; options keep favourable moves at the cost of premium.

Example. A hedger who wants upside buys an option.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Options cost nothing.

Margin and strategy risk

Short option legs require margin, and strategies must be assessed on net risk and capital needs.

Example. A short straddle needs substantial margin.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Spread positions need no margin.
