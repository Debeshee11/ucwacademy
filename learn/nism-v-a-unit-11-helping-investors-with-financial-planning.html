Key terms, examples and exam traps

Financial plan

A financial plan is a documented roadmap that links the client's goals, resources, risk profile and time horizon to a set of actions.

Example. A distributor prepares a written plan showing how each goal will be funded.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A financial plan is a list of schemes with last year's returns.

Goal-based planning

Goal-based planning sets investments against specific goals, such as a child's education, with a target amount, date and priority.

Example. A client earmarks a separate investment for a house purchase in seven years.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Goal-based planning ignores timelines.

Emergency fund

An emergency fund is money kept in safe and liquid assets, usually three to six months of expenses, for unexpected needs.

Example. A client keeps six months of living costs in a liquid fund.

Exam trap. This statement is false: An emergency fund should be invested in small-cap equity.

Life-cycle stage

A client's life stage, such as starting a career, raising a family or nearing retirement, shapes goals, risk capacity and asset allocation.

Example. A 28-year-old and a 58-year-old need different mixes for the same sum.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Life stage has no effect on investment choices.

Risk capacity versus tolerance

Risk capacity is the ability to bear loss financially, while risk tolerance is willingness to bear it emotionally; advice should respect the lower of the two.

Example. A wealthy client who panics at small losses has high capacity but low tolerance.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Risk tolerance and capacity are the same.

Time value of money

Money today is worth more than the same sum in the future because it can earn returns; planning must discount future needs and compound savings.

Example. A client needs ₹50 lakh in 15 years and works out what to invest today.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A rupee today has the same value as a rupee in ten years.

Retirement planning

Retirement planning estimates the corpus needed to meet expenses after earning stops, allowing for inflation and longevity.

Example. A client works out how much he must save each month to retire at 60.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Retirement planning needs no allowance for inflation.

Step-up SIP

A step-up SIP increases the instalment amount at regular intervals, usually yearly, in line with rising income.

Example. An investor raises her SIP by 10% every year as her salary grows.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A step-up SIP reduces the instalment each year.

Periodic review

A plan should be reviewed at least once a year and on major life events, and rebalanced to the target allocation.

Example. A client's portfolio is reviewed every year and after the birth of a child.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Once made, a plan never needs revisiting.
