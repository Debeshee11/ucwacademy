Key terms, examples and exam traps

Retirement corpus

The retirement corpus is the accumulated sum needed to fund expenses after retirement, estimated using expenses, inflation, years in retirement and returns.

Example. A client works out the lump sum she needs at 60.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The corpus is the amount spent each month.

Replacement ratio

The replacement ratio is the percentage of pre-retirement income needed after retirement to maintain the lifestyle, often taken as 60% to 80%.

Example. A client needs 70% of her final income in retirement.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The replacement ratio is more than 100% for everyone.

Longevity risk

Longevity risk is the risk of outliving one's savings because life expectancy is increasing.

Example. A retiree is still alive when savings run out.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Longevity risk is the risk of dying early.

Employees' Provident Fund

The EPF is a mandatory retirement savings scheme for salaried employees with contributions from employee and employer, and interest declared annually.

Example. A salaried person's monthly contribution is matched by her employer.

Exam trap. This statement is false: EPF is voluntary for all employees.

National Pension System

NPS is a voluntary defined-contribution scheme in which contributions are invested in equity, corporate bonds and government securities, with a part of the corpus used for an annuity.

Example. A person builds her pension through a regulated portable account.

Exam trap. This statement is false: NPS guarantees a fixed pension.

NPS Tier I and Tier II

Tier I is the retirement account with restricted withdrawals, while Tier II is a voluntary savings account with free withdrawals.

Example. A subscriber keeps a flexible withdrawal account in addition to the main account.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Tier I allows free withdrawal at any time.

Annuity options

Annuity options include life annuity, annuity with return of purchase price and joint-life annuity, each differing in payout and continuation.

Example. A couple selects an annuity that continues for the spouse after the first death.

Exam trap. This statement is false: All annuities pay the same.

Systematic withdrawal plan

A systematic withdrawal plan redeems a fixed amount from a fund at regular intervals to create retirement income.

Example. A retiree draws a monthly sum from a debt fund.

Exam trap. This statement is false: An SWP buys more units every month.

Safe withdrawal rate

A safe withdrawal rate is the percentage of the corpus that can be withdrawn each year with a low chance of running out, commonly assumed at around 4% in global studies.

Example. A client withdraws a modest percentage each year to make a corpus last.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A safe withdrawal rate is the rate of inflation.

Inflation in retirement

Inflation raises expenses over a long retirement, so some growth assets are needed even after retirement.

Example. A retiree's monthly cost of ₹50,000 rises over twenty years.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Retirees should avoid all growth assets.

Bucket strategy

A bucket strategy divides retirement money into near-term cash, medium-term income and long-term growth buckets.

Example. A retiree holds two years of expenses in cash and the rest invested for growth.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The bucket strategy puts everything in one asset.

Medical costs in retirement

Healthcare costs usually rise faster than general inflation, so retirement planning should include adequate health insurance.

Example. A retiree faces large medical expenses at 75.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Medical costs fall after retirement.

Pension from employer

A defined-benefit pension promises a fixed pension based on salary and service, while defined-contribution plans depend on contributions and returns.

Example. A government employee receives a pension formula-based on last salary.

Exam trap. This statement is false: In a defined-contribution plan the pension is guaranteed.

Reverse mortgage

A reverse mortgage lets senior citizens convert home equity into regular income while staying in the home, with the loan repaid from sale of the house later.

Example. A senior receives monthly payments against her house, and keeps living there.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A reverse mortgage requires moving out immediately.

Start early advantage

Starting early gives compounding more time, so a smaller monthly investment can build a larger corpus than a later start with a bigger sum.

Example. A 25-year-old needs far lower SIP than a 40-year-old for the same corpus.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Starting late gives the same result at the same SIP.

Retirement age assumptions

Retiring earlier needs a bigger corpus because savings must last more years with fewer years of accumulation.

Example. A person retiring at 50 needs a larger corpus than at 60.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Retiring earlier lowers the corpus needed.
