Key terms, examples and exam traps

Strategic asset allocation

Strategic allocation sets long-term target weights across asset classes based on goals and risk profile, with periodic rebalancing.

Example. A client's plan fixes 60% equity and 40% debt for years.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Strategic allocation changes with every market move.

Tactical asset allocation

Tactical allocation makes short-term shifts away from strategic weights to exploit perceived opportunities.

Example. An adviser temporarily reduces equity because valuations look stretched.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Tactical allocation is never allowed.

Rebalancing

Rebalancing restores the target allocation by selling what has grown overweight and buying what lags, controlling risk.

Example. Equity rises to 70% of a 60% target, so some is sold.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Rebalancing means buying only winners.

Core and satellite

A core and satellite approach keeps most assets in low-cost diversified holdings and a smaller part in higher-conviction ideas.

Example. A client holds 75% in index funds and 25% in thematic funds.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The satellite is the main holding.

Active versus passive investing

Active investing tries to beat a benchmark through selection; passive investing tracks it at low cost.

Example. An index fund follows the market rather than trying to beat it.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Active funds always beat passive funds.

Lump sum versus SIP

Lump sum investing puts money in at once while SIP spreads entry over time to average the cost and reduce timing risk.

Example. A client with a bonus splits it into monthly instalments.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A SIP always beats a lump sum.

Glide path

A glide path gradually moves a portfolio from growth assets to safer assets as a goal date approaches.

Example. As retirement nears, equity share is steadily lowered.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A glide path raises equity near the goal.

Investment policy statement

An investment policy statement documents the client's objectives, constraints, allocation and review rules, guiding long-term discipline.

Example. An adviser and client sign a document setting out targets and limits.

Exam trap. This statement is false: An investment policy statement is a promise of returns.

Behavioural biases

Biases such as overconfidence, loss aversion and herding lead investors to make poor decisions; advisers help counter them.

Example. A client sells in panic after a fall.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Investors always behave rationally.
