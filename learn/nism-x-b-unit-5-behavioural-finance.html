Key terms, examples and exam traps

Behavioural finance

It studies how psychology influences financial decisions and markets, in contrast to the view that investors are always rational.

Example. A client panics and sells in a fall.

Exam trap. This statement is false: It assumes investors always act rationally.

Prospect theory

Prospect theory says people value gains and losses relative to a reference point and feel losses more strongly than equal gains.

Example. A client dislikes a loss more than she likes the same gain.

Exam trap. This statement is false: It says people weigh gains and losses equally.

Loss aversion

Loss aversion is the tendency to feel the pain of a loss more than the pleasure of an equal gain.

Example. A client refuses to sell a stock at a loss.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Loss aversion makes clients take more risk.

Disposition effect

Investors tend to sell winners too early and hold losers too long.

Example. A client books a small profit but keeps a falling stock.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Investors sell losers quickly.

Anchoring

Anchoring is relying heavily on an initial number, like the purchase price, when making decisions.

Example. A client waits for a price to return to her buying level.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Anchoring is making decisions using fresh information.

Overconfidence

Overconfident investors overestimate their knowledge and ability, trade too often and under-diversify.

Example. A client believes she can time the market.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Overconfidence reduces trading.

Herding

Herding is following the actions of the crowd rather than one's own analysis.

Example. Clients rush into a rising stock because others do.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Herding means making independent decisions.

Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias is seeking information that supports existing beliefs and ignoring contrary evidence.

Example. A client reads only positive news on her holding.

Exam trap. This statement is false: It means testing views against evidence.

Recency bias

Recency bias gives too much weight to recent events and extrapolates them into the future.

Example. A client assumes last year's rally will continue.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Recency bias values long-term history.

Availability bias

Judging the likelihood of an event by how easily examples come to mind.

Example. A client avoids equity after hearing of a crash.

Exam trap. This statement is false: It means using full statistics.

Mental accounting

People treat money differently depending on its source or purpose, such as treating a bonus as free money.

Example. A client spends a windfall recklessly.

Exam trap. This statement is false: It treats all money as the same.

Endowment effect

People value what they own more than identical things they do not own.

Example. A client refuses to sell an inherited stock.

Exam trap. This statement is false: It means pricing assets at market value.

Status quo bias

A preference to keep things as they are and avoid change.

Example. A client leaves money idle in a savings account.

Exam trap. This statement is false: It drives clients to rebalance often.

Regret aversion

Avoiding decisions that may cause regret, leading to inaction or following the crowd.

Example. A client delays investing for fear of a fall.

Exam trap. This statement is false: It makes clients eager to take bold bets.

Framing

The way information is presented influences the decision, even when facts are the same.

Example. A fund described as 90% success is liked more than one with 10% failure.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Framing has no effect on choices.

Cognitive and emotional biases

Cognitive biases arise from faulty reasoning and can be reduced with information, while emotional biases arise from feelings and are harder to correct, so the adviser adapts to them.

Example. An adviser accepts an emotional bias and designs around it.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Emotional biases are easy to remove with data.

Managing client behaviour

Advisers help by setting a written policy, pre-agreeing rebalancing rules and using goal-based buckets to reduce emotional decisions.

Example. A written plan guides a client during a fall.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Advisers should follow every client impulse.

Self-attribution

Crediting success to skill and failure to bad luck.

Example. An investor claims her gains came from her analysis only.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Self-attribution leads to learning from errors.
