Key terms, examples and exam traps

Porter's five forces

Porter's model assesses industry attractiveness by competitive rivalry, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of buyers and suppliers.

Example. An analyst evaluates how easy it is for rivals to enter and compete.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Porter's model measures a company's profit margin.

Barriers to entry

Barriers to entry, such as capital needs, regulation, brands and scale, protect existing firms from new competitors.

Example. A telecom sector needs spectrum and heavy capital.

Exam trap. This statement is false: High barriers to entry make an industry easy to enter.

Industry life cycle

Industries pass through start-up, growth, maturity and decline stages, with different growth rates and risks.

Example. A new technology sector is in rapid growth with high uncertainty.

Exam trap. This statement is false: All industries remain in the growth stage.

Cyclical industry

A cyclical industry's earnings rise and fall sharply with the economic cycle, as in steel, autos and capital goods.

Example. A steel company's profits swing with the economy.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Cyclical industries have stable earnings in all conditions.

Defensive industry

A defensive industry sells essentials with stable demand, such as FMCG, utilities and pharma, and holds up better in downturns.

Example. A toothpaste maker's sales remain steady in a recession.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Defensive industries are the most sensitive to the economic cycle.

Industry concentration

Concentration measures how much of the market is held by a few firms; the Herfindahl-Hirschman index is one such measure.

Example. Three firms hold 80% of the market, indicating concentration.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A concentrated industry has many equal small players.

Cost structure

Operating leverage depends on fixed versus variable costs; high fixed costs magnify profit swings as volume changes.

Example. A firm with high fixed costs sees profits jump sharply when sales rise.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Fixed costs change in proportion to sales.

Substitute threat

Substitutes limit the prices an industry can charge, as customers can switch to alternatives.

Example. Rising bus fares push travellers to trains.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Substitutes have no effect on pricing.

Regulation and policy

Government policy, taxes and regulation can change industry profitability, such as price controls in pharma or spectrum charges in telecom.

Example. A price cap reduces margins in a regulated industry.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Regulation never affects industry profits.

PESTLE analysis

PESTLE reviews political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental factors that affect an industry.

Example. An analyst lists the policy, tech and legal drivers for a sector.

Exam trap. This statement is false: PESTLE is a ratio of price to earnings.

Market share

Market share is a company's sales as a percentage of the industry total and shows competitive strength over time.

Example. A firm's share rises from 20% to 25% over three years.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Market share is the same as profit margin.

Capacity utilisation

Capacity utilisation shows the share of installed capacity actually used; low utilisation signals weak demand or excess capacity.

Example. A cement industry running at 60% capacity has spare supply.

Exam trap. This statement is false: High capacity utilisation always signals weak demand.

SWOT analysis

SWOT lists a company's strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats to summarise its position.

Example. An analyst lists internal strengths and external risks in one table.

Exam trap. This statement is false: SWOT is a method of calculating cost of capital.
