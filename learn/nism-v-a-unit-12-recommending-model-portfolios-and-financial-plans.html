Key terms, examples and exam traps

Asset allocation

Asset allocation is the division of a portfolio among asset classes such as equity, debt and gold, and is the main driver of risk and return over time.

Example. A client's portfolio is split 60% equity, 30% debt and 10% gold.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Asset allocation matters less than picking the best fund.

Strategic asset allocation

Strategic asset allocation sets long-term target weights based on the client's profile, which are maintained by periodic rebalancing.

Example. A client's plan says 60% equity for the next ten years, whatever the news.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Strategic allocation changes with every market move.

Tactical asset allocation

Tactical allocation makes short-term deviations from the strategic weights to take advantage of market conditions.

Example. A manager temporarily raises debt above target because equities look expensive.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Tactical allocation means never deviating from target.

Rebalancing

Rebalancing means bringing the portfolio back to its target allocation by selling what has grown too large and buying what has fallen behind.

Example. Equity has risen to 70% of the portfolio against a 60% target, so some is sold.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Rebalancing means adding only to the best performer.

Core and satellite

A core and satellite approach holds the bulk of the portfolio in stable, low-cost funds and a smaller satellite part in higher-conviction ideas.

Example. A portfolio holds 70% in index funds and 30% in a thematic fund.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Core and satellite means holding only satellite ideas.

Age-based rule of thumb

A rough guide sets equity at 100 minus the investor's age, adjusting for risk profile; it is a starting point, not a rule.

Example. A 40-year-old starts discussing about 60% equity.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The rule fixes the exact equity percentage for everyone of a given age.

Glide path

A glide path gradually reduces equity and raises safer assets as a goal date approaches, to protect accumulated gains.

Example. As a child's education nears, a family shifts from equity to debt funds.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A glide path raises equity as the goal date approaches.

Documenting advice

The distributor should record the client's profile, the recommendation and the reasons, and obtain the client's acknowledgement to show suitability.

Example. A distributor files a signed risk profile and rationale for the recommended funds.

Exam trap. This statement is false: No written record of advice is needed.

Model portfolio

A model portfolio is a pre-defined allocation for a risk profile, used as a template and customised for the client.

Example. A distributor shows a typical moderate-risk allocation as a starting point.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A model portfolio guarantees returns.

Conflict of interest

A conflict of interest arises when the distributor's earnings could bias the advice; it must be disclosed and managed in the client's favour.

Example. A distributor discloses that one scheme pays him more but recommends the better fit for the client.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Conflicts of interest need not be disclosed.
