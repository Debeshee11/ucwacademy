Key terms, examples and exam traps

Commodity price drivers

Commodity prices are driven by supply, demand, inventories, weather, policy and the currency.

Example. A poor monsoon cuts crop output and raises prices.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Commodity prices depend only on company earnings.

Stock-to-use ratio

The stock-to-use ratio compares closing stocks with consumption; low levels signal tightness and price pressure.

Example. Inventories fall to a few weeks of consumption and prices rise.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A low stock-to-use ratio signals oversupply.

Seasonality

Agricultural commodities show seasonal price patterns tied to harvests and demand cycles.

Example. Prices of a crop tend to fall after harvest.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Seasonality is unimportant for agricultural prices.

Cost of carry in commodities

The cost of carry includes storage, insurance and financing costs, and links futures prices to spot prices.

Example. A commodity future trades above spot by the cost of storing it.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Cost of carry has no effect on futures prices.

Gold drivers

Gold prices are influenced by real interest rates, the US dollar, central bank buying, inflation expectations and risk sentiment.

Example. Gold rises when real interest rates fall.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Gold falls whenever inflation expectations rise.

Crude oil drivers

Crude oil prices are driven by OPEC+ decisions, global demand, inventories and geopolitical events.

Example. Production cuts by major producers raise prices.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Crude oil prices are unaffected by supply decisions.

Currency link

Many commodities are priced in dollars, so a weaker rupee raises their domestic prices, other things equal.

Example. Imported edible oil gets costlier when the rupee falls.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The rupee has no effect on domestic commodity prices.

Futures curve

The futures curve plots prices across expiries; contango signals plentiful supply and backwardation signals tightness.

Example. Near-month prices exceed far-month prices when supply is short.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Backwardation means plentiful supply.
