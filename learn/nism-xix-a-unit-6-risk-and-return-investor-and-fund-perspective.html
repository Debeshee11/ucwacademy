Key terms, examples and exam traps

Liquidity risk in AIFs

AIF investors cannot redeem on demand; capital is locked for the tenure and exit depends on the fund's realisations.

Example. An investor needs cash but the fund has two more years to run.

Exam trap. This statement is false: AIF units can be redeemed any day.

Valuation risk

Unlisted assets are valued by models and judgement, so reported NAV can differ from realised value.

Example. A fund's marked value drops when the asset is sold for less.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Valuation risk does not arise in private assets.

Concentration risk

AIFs often hold a small number of investments, so one failure can have a large impact on returns.

Example. A fund with ten investments loses heavily when one fails.

Exam trap. This statement is false: AIFs are always well diversified across hundreds of assets.

Manager and key-person risk

Returns depend on the skill and continuity of the manager's team; the departure of key people can damage a fund.

Example. A star investor leaves the fund house.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Manager risk does not matter for AIFs.

Leverage risk

Use of debt, at fund or investee level, amplifies gains and losses and may cause distress in downturns.

Example. An investee with heavy debt struggles when rates rise.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Leverage lowers the risk of losses.

Exit risk

The returns of a private fund rely on exits via IPO, sale or buyback, which depend on market conditions and may be delayed.

Example. A fund cannot list its investee because markets are weak.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Exit is guaranteed at the fund's tenure.

Regulatory and tax risk

Changes in law or tax treatment can reduce net returns or alter the structure.

Example. A change in tax rules reduces after-tax returns.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Regulations never change for AIFs.

Return expectations

Alternatives aim to deliver higher returns than listed markets as compensation for illiquidity and risk, but outcomes vary widely.

Example. A PE fund targets a premium over equity indices.

Exam trap. This statement is false: AIFs guarantee a return above listed markets.

Risk-adjusted performance

Because private assets are valued infrequently, volatility looks low; investors should look at drawdowns, dispersion and realised returns.

Example. An advisor looks at realised returns instead of reported NAV smoothness.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Low reported volatility proves low risk.

Dispersion of returns

The difference between top and bottom funds is large in private markets, so manager selection matters more than in listed funds.

Example. The best PE fund greatly outperforms the worst.

Exam trap. This statement is false: All funds in a category perform alike.

Fees and net returns

Management fees, carry and expenses reduce net returns, so investors compare returns after all costs.

Example. A fund's gross multiple looks good but net is lower after fees.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Gross returns are what investors receive.

Concentration limits as risk control

Regulatory limits on single-investee exposure help to reduce concentration risk in Category I and II AIFs.

Example. A fund cannot put more than 25% in one company.

Exam trap. This statement is false: There are no limits on a single investment.
