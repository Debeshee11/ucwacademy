Key terms, examples and exam traps

Discounted cash flow

DCF values a business as the present value of its expected future free cash flows, discounted at the required rate of return.

Example. An analyst projects cash flows for ten years and discounts them to today.

Exam trap. This statement is false: DCF values a business by multiplying earnings by a ratio.

Free cash flow to firm

FCFF is the cash available to all capital providers after operating costs and investment, discounted at WACC to value the firm.

Example. An analyst computes cash available to both lenders and shareholders.

Exam trap. This statement is false: FCFF is cash available only to shareholders.

Free cash flow to equity

FCFE is the cash available to shareholders after debt payments and reinvestment, discounted at the cost of equity.

Example. An analyst values the equity using cash left after servicing debt.

Exam trap. This statement is false: FCFE is discounted at WACC.

WACC

The weighted average cost of capital blends the cost of equity and after-tax cost of debt in proportion to their weights, and is the discount rate for FCFF.

Example. A firm funded 60% by equity at 12% and 40% by debt at 8% (after tax 6%) has a blended cost.

Exam trap. This statement is false: WACC uses only the cost of equity.

Cost of equity (CAPM)

CAPM estimates the cost of equity as the risk-free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium.

Example. With a risk-free rate of 6%, beta of 1.2 and market return of 12%, the required return is 13.2%.

Exam trap. This statement is false: CAPM estimates cost of debt.

Terminal value

Terminal value represents the value of cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period, often a large share of total DCF value.

Example. An analyst assumes cash flows grow steadily forever after year ten.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Terminal value is ignored in DCF.

Dividend discount model

The DDM values a share as the present value of expected dividends; in the Gordon model, value = D1 ÷ (r − g).

Example. A share paying ₹5 next year with growth 5% and required return 15% is valued at ₹50.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The DDM values shares using book value.

Price-to-earnings multiple

P/E divides price by earnings per share and is a relative valuation tool, compared with peers and history.

Example. A stock at 25 times earnings is compared with sector peers.

Exam trap. This statement is false: P/E is price divided by book value.

Price-to-book multiple

P/B compares market price with book value per share, widely used for banks and asset-heavy firms.

Example. A bank trading at 2.5 times book value.

Exam trap. This statement is false: P/B is used mainly for software firms.

EV/EBITDA

EV/EBITDA compares enterprise value with operating earnings before depreciation, neutral to capital structure, useful for comparing leveraged firms.

Example. A firm with EV of ₹5,000 crore and EBITDA of ₹500 crore trades at 10 times.

Exam trap. This statement is false: EV/EBITDA ignores debt entirely.

Enterprise value

Enterprise value is market capitalisation plus debt minus cash, the price of the whole business.

Example. A company with market cap ₹1,000 crore, debt ₹300 crore and cash ₹100 crore has EV of ₹1,200 crore.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Enterprise value equals market capitalisation.

PEG ratio

The PEG ratio divides the P/E by the expected earnings growth rate, adjusting the multiple for growth.

Example. A P/E of 30 with growth of 15% gives a PEG of 2.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The PEG ratio is the P/E multiplied by growth.

Relative valuation

Relative valuation values a security by comparing multiples with those of similar companies or its own history.

Example. An analyst compares a bank's P/B with peers.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Relative valuation requires projecting cash flows.

Sum-of-the-parts

Sum-of-the-parts values each business unit separately and adds them, useful for conglomerates.

Example. An analyst values a group's three divisions separately and sums them.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Sum-of-the-parts uses only a single multiple.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis shows how the valuation changes when key assumptions such as growth or discount rate are altered.

Example. An analyst shows value at different discount rates.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Sensitivity analysis fixes all assumptions.

Intrinsic versus market value

A recommendation compares intrinsic value with market price: buy if undervalued, sell if overvalued, after allowing a margin of safety.

Example. An analyst recommends buying when the market price is below fair value.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Market value always equals intrinsic value.

Equity risk premium

The equity risk premium is the extra return investors require from equities over the risk-free rate.

Example. Investors demand 6% more than government bonds for equity risk.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The equity risk premium equals the risk-free rate.

Book value

Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding, from the balance sheet.

Example. Equity of ₹500 crore on 10 crore shares is ₹50 per share.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Book value per share is the market price.

Cyclically adjusted earnings

For cyclical firms, valuation based on peak or trough earnings misleads, so analysts use average or normalised earnings.

Example. An analyst uses mid-cycle earnings for a steel company.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Peak earnings are always the right base for cyclical stocks.
