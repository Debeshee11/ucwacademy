Key terms, examples and exam traps

Market risk

Market risk is the risk of loss from overall movements in market prices that cannot be diversified away.

Example. A portfolio falls because the whole market fell.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Market risk can be eliminated by diversification.

Credit risk

Credit risk is the chance that a borrower will not repay interest or principal on time.

Example. A bond issuer defaults on interest payments.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Credit risk applies only to equity shares.

Interest rate risk

Interest rate risk is the risk that changes in interest rates will affect the value of fixed-income investments; prices fall when rates rise.

Example. A bond portfolio loses value after the policy rate is raised.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Bond prices rise when interest rates rise.

Inflation risk

Inflation risk is the risk that returns will not keep pace with price rises, eroding purchasing power.

Example. A saver's 5% deposit returns less than the 6% inflation.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Inflation risk affects only equity investors.

Liquidity risk

Liquidity risk is the risk of being unable to sell an asset quickly at a fair price.

Example. A client cannot sell a plot of land at short notice.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Liquidity risk is the chance of a price fall.

Reinvestment risk

Reinvestment risk is the risk that cash flows such as coupons can only be reinvested at lower rates than the original.

Example. A bond's coupons are reinvested at lower rates after rates fall.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Reinvestment risk is the risk of default.

Systematic and unsystematic risk

Systematic risk affects the whole market and cannot be diversified; unsystematic risk is specific to a company or sector and can be reduced by diversification.

Example. A strike at one company is specific risk, while a recession affects all.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Systematic risk can be diversified away.

Standard deviation

Standard deviation measures how widely returns vary around the average and is used as a measure of total risk.

Example. A fund with a standard deviation of 20% is more volatile than one with 10%.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A higher standard deviation indicates lower risk.

Beta

Beta measures a security's sensitivity to market moves; beta above 1 means more volatile than the market.

Example. A stock with a beta of 1.4 tends to move more than the market.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A beta of 1 means the stock never moves.

Diversification

Diversification reduces unsystematic risk by holding assets that do not move together, such as equity, debt and gold.

Example. An investor spreads money across asset classes and sectors.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Diversification eliminates all risk.

Risk tolerance

Risk tolerance is a client's willingness to accept fluctuations; it differs from risk capacity, which is the ability to bear loss.

Example. A client who loses sleep over a small fall has low tolerance.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Risk tolerance is a fixed number set by regulation.

Risk management process

Risk management involves identifying risks, measuring them, choosing a treatment such as avoiding, reducing, transferring or retaining and monitoring.

Example. A family buys health insurance to transfer the risk of a large medical bill.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Risk management means ignoring all risks.

Value at Risk

Value at Risk estimates the maximum likely loss over a period at a given confidence level under normal conditions.

Example. A portfolio's one-day 95% VaR is ₹2 lakh.

Exam trap. This statement is false: VaR is the worst possible loss ever.

Risk-adjusted return

Risk-adjusted measures such as the Sharpe ratio show return earned per unit of risk taken.

Example. Two funds with equal returns are compared on how much risk each took.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The highest-return fund always has the best risk-adjusted performance.
