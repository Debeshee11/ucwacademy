Key terms, examples and exam traps

Portfolio management process

The process includes planning with the client's objectives, executing the allocation and selection, and then feedback through monitoring and rebalancing.

Example. A manager reviews a portfolio each quarter.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The process ends when securities are purchased.

Investment policy statement

The investment policy statement records objectives, constraints, risk profile and guidelines, and governs the manager's decisions.

Example. A client's IPS limits equity to 60%.

Exam trap. This statement is false: An IPS is rewritten for every trade.

Strategic asset allocation

Strategic allocation sets long-term target weights across asset classes, based on objectives and risk, and is reviewed infrequently.

Example. A target of 60% equity and 40% debt.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Strategic allocation changes with every market move.

Tactical asset allocation

Tactical allocation makes short-term deviations from strategic weights to exploit market views.

Example. A manager holds extra cash when valuations are stretched.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Tactical allocation is the long-term target.

Correlation

Correlation measures how two returns move together, and combining assets with low correlation reduces portfolio risk.

Example. Gold and equity often move differently.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Combining assets cannot reduce risk.

Portfolio return

The expected return of a portfolio is the weighted average of the expected returns of its assets.

Example. 50% at 10% and 50% at 6% gives 8%.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Portfolio return is the simple sum of asset returns.

Portfolio variance

Portfolio variance depends on each asset's risk, its weight and the correlation between assets, so it is not a simple average of risks.

Example. Two risky assets with low correlation give a portfolio less risky than the average.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Portfolio risk is always the weighted average of the asset risks.

Efficient frontier

The efficient frontier shows portfolios that give the highest expected return for each level of risk.

Example. A portfolio below the frontier gives too little return for its risk.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Every portfolio lies on the efficient frontier.

Risk-averse investor

A risk-averse investor needs more expected return to accept more risk and prefers the less risky choice for the same return.

Example. An investor picks the safer of two equal-return options.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A risk-averse investor ignores risk.

Diversification

Diversification spreads capital across assets so that specific risk falls, but it does not remove market risk.

Example. Holding 30 stocks lowers company-specific risk.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Diversification removes all risk.

Systematic and unsystematic risk

Systematic risk affects the whole market and cannot be diversified, while unsystematic risk is specific to a company or sector and can be.

Example. A recession hits all stocks.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Unsystematic risk cannot be reduced by diversification.
