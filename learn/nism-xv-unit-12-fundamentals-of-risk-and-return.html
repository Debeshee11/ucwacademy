Key terms, examples and exam traps

Expected return

Expected return is the probability-weighted average of possible returns of an investment.

Example. An asset has a 50% chance of 20% and a 50% chance of 0%, giving 10%.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Expected return is the highest possible return.

Variance and standard deviation

Variance measures the dispersion of returns around the mean; standard deviation is its square root and the common measure of volatility.

Example. A stock's returns swing widely around its average.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Standard deviation is the average return.

Correlation

Correlation measures how two assets move together, from −1 to +1; lower correlation gives greater diversification benefit.

Example. Two assets with correlation near zero reduce portfolio volatility when combined.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A correlation of +1 gives the best diversification.

Systematic risk and beta

Systematic risk is market-wide and measured by beta; it cannot be diversified away.

Example. A stock with beta 1.3 amplifies market moves.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Beta measures company-specific risk.

CAPM

The Capital Asset Pricing Model states that expected return equals the risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium.

Example. A stock's required return is built from risk-free rate and market risk premium.

Exam trap. This statement is false: CAPM says return depends on total risk.

Security market line

The security market line plots expected return against beta; securities above it are undervalued.

Example. A stock offering more return than CAPM predicts lies above the line.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The security market line plots return against standard deviation.

Sharpe ratio

The Sharpe ratio is excess return over the risk-free rate divided by standard deviation, measuring return per unit of total risk.

Example. A fund earns 14% with risk-free 6% and standard deviation 16%, giving 0.5.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The Sharpe ratio uses beta as the risk measure.

Treynor ratio

The Treynor ratio is excess return divided by beta, measuring return per unit of systematic risk.

Example. A portfolio's excess return is divided by its beta.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The Treynor ratio uses standard deviation.

Jensen's alpha

Jensen's alpha is the return in excess of the CAPM-predicted return, showing a manager's value addition.

Example. A fund earns 2% more than CAPM predicts.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A negative alpha indicates outperformance.

Efficient frontier

The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios offering the highest expected return for each level of risk.

Example. A set of optimal portfolios that cannot be improved on both return and risk.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Every portfolio lies on the efficient frontier.

Risk-free rate

The risk-free rate is the return on an investment with no default risk, such as the government security yield.

Example. A ten-year G-sec yield is used as the base rate.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The risk-free rate is the rate on corporate bonds.

Diversification benefit

Combining imperfectly correlated assets reduces portfolio risk without a proportional fall in return.

Example. Adding gold to an equity portfolio smooths overall volatility.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Diversification increases portfolio risk.

Downside risk

Downside risk measures the chance and size of losses below a target, as in semi-deviation or maximum drawdown.

Example. An investor tracks how far a fund fell from its peak.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Downside risk counts only positive returns.

Maximum drawdown

Maximum drawdown is the largest peak-to-trough fall in value over a period, showing the worst loss an investor would have faced.

Example. A fund fell 35% from its high before recovering.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Maximum drawdown is the highest gain.

Q1. Which statement best describes “Sharpe ratio”?

  1. The debt-to-equity ratio is total debt divided by shareholders' equity and shows financial leverage.
  2. The Sharpe ratio is excess return over the risk-free rate divided by standard deviation, measuring return per unit of total risk.
  3. Fundamental analysis estimates a security's intrinsic value from economic, industry and company factors and compares it with market price.
  4. Sum-of-the-parts values each business unit separately and adds them, useful for conglomerates.

Q2. Which statement best describes “Downside risk”?

  1. Dow theory holds that markets move in primary, secondary and minor trends, and that averages must confirm each other.
  2. For cyclical firms, valuation based on peak or trough earnings misleads, so analysts use average or normalised earnings.
  3. Asset turnover is revenue divided by total assets and shows how efficiently assets generate sales.
  4. Downside risk measures the chance and size of losses below a target, as in semi-deviation or maximum drawdown.

Q3. Which statement best describes “Risk-free rate”?

  1. The risk-free rate is the return on an investment with no default risk, such as the government security yield.
  2. A candlestick shows open, high, low and close for a period, with the body showing the open-to-close range and wicks showing extremes.
  3. Crude oil prices are driven by OPEC+ decisions, global demand, inventories and geopolitical events.
  4. Interest coverage is EBIT divided by interest expense, showing how comfortably a company can pay interest.

Q4. Which statement best describes “Jensen's alpha”?

  1. Jensen's alpha is the return in excess of the CAPM-predicted return, showing a manager's value addition.
  2. The ex-dividend date is the date from which a share trades without the right to the declared dividend; buyers on or after it do not receive it.
  3. The Purchasing Managers' Index surveys business activity; a reading above 50 indicates expansion and below 50 contraction.
  4. A demerger splits a business into two or more entities, and shareholders receive shares in the new entity in a given ratio.

Q5. Which term is described here? The Treynor ratio is excess return divided by beta, measuring return per unit of systematic risk.

  1. Margin of safety
  2. Free cash flow to firm
  3. Treynor ratio
  4. Bonus issue
