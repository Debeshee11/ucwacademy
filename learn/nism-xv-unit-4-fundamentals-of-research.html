Key terms, examples and exam traps

Top-down approach

The top-down approach starts with the economy, then selects industries and finally companies.

Example. An analyst first judges growth, then picks sectors, then picks stocks.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A top-down approach starts with a single stock.

Bottom-up approach

The bottom-up approach starts with individual company analysis and gives less weight to macro and sector views.

Example. An analyst picks strong businesses irrespective of sector or economy.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A bottom-up approach starts with the global economy.

Fundamental analysis

Fundamental analysis estimates a security's intrinsic value from economic, industry and company factors and compares it with market price.

Example. An analyst studies earnings and cash flow to judge fair value.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Fundamental analysis relies only on price charts.

Technical analysis

Technical analysis studies price and volume history to forecast future price moves, assuming patterns repeat.

Example. An analyst studies charts and indicators rather than financial statements.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Technical analysis studies a company's balance sheet.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative analysis assesses non-numerical factors such as management quality, brand, competitive position and governance.

Example. An analyst evaluates the integrity and track record of the management.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Qualitative analysis uses only financial ratios.

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative analysis uses numerical data and models, such as ratios, forecasts and statistics, to evaluate securities.

Example. An analyst builds a forecast model of revenue and profits.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Quantitative analysis ignores numbers.

Intrinsic value

Intrinsic value is the estimated true worth of a security based on fundamentals, which may differ from market price.

Example. A stock priced at ₹80 is estimated to be worth ₹110.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Intrinsic value is today's market price.

Primary and secondary data

Primary data is collected directly by the analyst, such as management interviews and channel checks; secondary data is taken from existing sources like filings.

Example. An analyst visits a plant to gather information first-hand.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Annual reports are primary data collected by the analyst.

Research process

The research process involves defining the objective, collecting data, analysing, valuing, forming a view, writing and monitoring.

Example. An analyst follows steps from data gathering to publishing and updating.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The research process ends with the first report.

Margin of safety

Margin of safety is the gap between intrinsic value and the price paid, providing a cushion against errors in estimation.

Example. An analyst buys only when price is well below estimated value.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A margin of safety means paying more than intrinsic value.
