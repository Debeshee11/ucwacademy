Key terms, examples and exam traps

Coupon and face value

A bond's coupon is the interest paid on its face value, and the face value is repaid at maturity.

Example. A 8% bond of ₹1,000 face pays ₹80 a year.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The coupon is a share of the bond's market price.

Price and yield

Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions, so a rise in market yields lowers the price.

Example. Rates rise and an existing bond loses value.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Bond prices rise when market yields rise.

Yield to maturity

YTM is the single discount rate that equates the bond's price to the present value of its cash flows if held to maturity.

Example. A bond bought below par has a YTM above its coupon.

Exam trap. This statement is false: YTM is always equal to the coupon rate.

Current yield

Current yield is annual coupon divided by the current market price.

Example. A ₹80 coupon on a bond priced at ₹950 gives a current yield above 8%.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Current yield includes the capital gain at maturity.

Duration

Duration measures the sensitivity of a bond's price to interest rate changes, and a longer duration means more rate risk.

Example. A long bond falls more than a short bond when yields rise.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Duration measures credit risk.

Credit risk

Credit risk is the risk that the issuer fails to pay interest or principal, which credit ratings help assess.

Example. An investor requires a higher yield on a lower-rated bond.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Credit risk is the same for all bonds of a firm's group.

Reinvestment risk

Reinvestment risk is the chance that coupons are reinvested at lower rates than the original yield.

Example. Falling rates reduce income from reinvested coupons.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Reinvestment risk applies only to zero-coupon bonds.

Zero-coupon bond

A zero-coupon bond pays no periodic interest and is issued at a discount to face value, repaying face value at maturity.

Example. A bond bought at ₹700 repays ₹1,000.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A zero-coupon bond pays a high monthly coupon.

Yield curve

The yield curve plots yields against maturity, and an upward-sloping curve means longer maturities have higher yields.

Example. Ten-year yields are above one-year yields.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A normal yield curve slopes downward.

Government securities

Government securities carry sovereign backing and little credit risk, and set the benchmark for other yields.

Example. Corporate bond spreads are measured over a G-sec yield.

Exam trap. This statement is false: G-secs carry high default risk.
