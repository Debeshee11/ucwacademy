Key terms, examples and exam traps

Covered call

A covered call holds the underlying and sells a call against it, earning the premium but capping the upside.

Example. An investor holding shares sells a call to earn income.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A covered call has unlimited upside.

Protective put

A protective put buys a put against a held position, setting a floor under the loss at the cost of the premium.

Example. An investor buys a put to insure shares she owns.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A protective put adds to the loss when prices fall.

Bull call spread

A bull call spread buys a lower-strike call and sells a higher-strike call, limiting both the cost and the profit.

Example. A trader buys a 500 call and sells a 520 call.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A bull call spread has unlimited profit.

Long straddle

A long straddle buys a call and a put at the same strike and expiry, profiting from a large move in either direction.

Example. A trader expects a big move after results but not its direction.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A long straddle profits if the price stays still.

Long strangle

A long strangle buys an out-of-the-money call and an out-of-the-money put, costing less than a straddle but needing a bigger move.

Example. A trader buys a 520 call and a 480 put.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A long strangle uses the same strike for both options.

Collar

A collar holds the underlying, buys a put and sells a call, limiting both downside and upside, often at a low net cost.

Example. An investor protects shares with a put financed by selling a call.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A collar leaves both ends of risk unlimited.

Bear put spread

A bear put spread buys a higher-strike put and sells a lower-strike put, profiting from a moderate fall with limited risk.

Example. A trader buys a 500 put and sells a 480 put.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A bear put spread profits from a rise.

Butterfly spread

A butterfly spread uses three strikes to profit if the price stays near the middle strike, with limited risk and limited reward.

Example. A trader expects little movement and uses three strikes.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A butterfly spread profits from a large move.
