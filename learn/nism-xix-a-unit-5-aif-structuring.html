Key terms, examples and exam traps

Trust structure

Most AIFs are set up as trusts, with a trustee holding the fund's assets for investors as beneficiaries, giving flexibility and pass-through tax treatment.

Example. A fund is a trust with a trustee and beneficiaries.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Most AIFs are public limited companies.

LLP and company forms

An AIF can also be set up as a limited liability partnership or a company, but trusts are the most common form.

Example. A fund is structured as an LLP.

Exam trap. This statement is false: An AIF can only be a trust.

Sponsor

The sponsor is the person who sets up the AIF, and typically contributes capital and appoints the manager.

Example. The firm that creates the fund and invests its own money.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The sponsor is an investor with no role in formation.

Investment manager

The investment manager manages the fund's portfolio and operations under an investment management agreement, earning fees.

Example. A company makes the investment decisions of the fund.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The manager is an investor in the fund.

Trustee

The trustee holds the fund's assets in trust and is responsible for oversight and ensuring the fund follows its documents.

Example. An entity ensures the trust deed is complied with.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The trustee decides which companies to invest in.

Fund of funds

A fund of funds invests in other AIFs rather than directly in companies, adding diversification but another layer of fees.

Example. A Category II fund invests in several PE funds.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A fund of funds invests directly in start-ups.

Feeder fund

A feeder fund collects money from investors and invests it in a master fund, often used for foreign investors or to pool distributions.

Example. A fund channels investor money into a larger master fund.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A feeder fund invests in listed shares only.

Scheme versus fund

An AIF can launch multiple schemes under one registration, each with its own investors, strategy and tenure.

Example. A registered fund offers a new scheme every year.

Exam trap. This statement is false: An AIF can launch only one scheme.

Co-investment

Co-investment lets selected investors invest directly alongside the fund in a deal, often with lower fees, subject to regulatory conditions.

Example. An investor is offered a direct stake in a deal alongside the fund.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Co-investment is prohibited.

GIFT City funds

Funds set up in GIFT City's IFSC can pool money in foreign currency and are governed by a separate regulatory framework.

Example. A fund set up in the international financial centre raises dollar capital.

Exam trap. This statement is false: GIFT City funds are restricted to domestic rupee investing.

Pass-through

In a pass-through structure, income is taxed in the investors' hands rather than at the fund level, preserving the character of income.

Example. Investors are taxed on the fund's capital gains directly.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Pass-through means the fund pays all the tax.

Side pocket

A side pocket separates illiquid or distressed assets from the main portfolio so that exits and fees of other investors are not affected.

Example. A fund isolates a stuck investment for later realisation.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A side pocket merges all assets into one pool.
