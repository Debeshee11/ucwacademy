Key terms, examples and exam traps

Absolute return

Absolute return is the percentage change in value over a period, ignoring how long it took. It is (end value − start value) ÷ start value.

Example. A ₹10,000 investment grows to ₹12,000 in total, and the gain is expressed as 20%.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Absolute return already adjusts for the length of time.

CAGR

Compound Annual Growth Rate is the constant yearly rate at which an investment would have grown to reach its final value, used for periods over one year.

Example. ₹1 lakh becomes ₹1.21 lakh in two years, which is 10% a year compounded.

Exam trap. This statement is false: CAGR is the simple average of each year's return.

XIRR

XIRR is the annualised return when there are multiple cash flows on different dates, such as SIP instalments.

Example. An investor wants the true annual return on a SIP made across many months.

Exam trap. This statement is false: XIRR can be used only for lump-sum investments.

Standard deviation

Standard deviation measures how much a fund's returns vary around its average. A higher figure means more volatility.

Example. Two funds earn the same average return, but one swings far more from year to year.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A higher standard deviation means lower risk.

Beta

Beta measures a fund's sensitivity to the market. A beta of 1.2 means it is expected to move 20% more than the market.

Example. A fund tends to rise 12% when the market rises 10%.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Beta measures the fund's absolute return.

Alpha

Alpha is the excess return of a fund over what its risk (beta) would predict, showing the value added by the fund manager.

Example. A fund earns 3% more than a CAPM-based expectation for its risk.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A negative alpha shows outperformance.

Sharpe ratio

The Sharpe ratio is the excess return over the risk-free rate per unit of standard deviation. A higher ratio means better risk-adjusted performance.

Example. Two funds have the same return, but one takes less volatility to achieve it and has the higher ratio.

Exam trap. This statement is false: The Sharpe ratio uses beta as its risk measure.

Tracking error

Tracking error is the standard deviation of the difference between an index fund's return and its benchmark's. A lower figure means closer replication.

Example. An index fund drifts a little from its index return every year.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A high tracking error is desirable for index funds.

Total Return Index (TRI)

A TRI reflects both price changes and reinvested dividends of the index constituents, and is the proper benchmark for comparison with a fund's returns.

Example. A fund's return is compared with an index that includes dividends.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A price index is the preferred benchmark because it includes dividends.

Modified duration

Modified duration estimates the percentage change in a bond's price for a 1% change in yield. A higher duration means more interest-rate sensitivity.

Example. A bond fund with a duration of 5 falls about 5% if yields rise 1%.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Duration measures the credit rating of the bond.

Yield to maturity (YTM)

YTM is the annualised return earned if a bond is held to maturity and all coupons are reinvested at the same rate.

Example. A bond's expected return if held until it matures is called its yield.

Exam trap. This statement is false: YTM is the coupon rate printed on the bond.

Credit risk

Credit risk is the chance that a bond issuer fails to pay interest or principal on time. Lower-rated issuers carry higher credit risk.

Example. A fund holding a low-rated company's bonds suffers when the company defaults.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Government securities carry the highest credit risk.

Interest rate risk

When interest rates rise, the prices of existing bonds fall and vice versa, which affects debt fund NAVs, especially of longer-duration funds.

Example. A long-duration gilt fund loses value when the central bank raises rates.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Bond prices rise when interest rates rise.

Risk-return trade-off

Higher potential return generally comes with higher risk. Investors who want higher returns must accept greater volatility.

Example. An investor wanting equity-like returns must accept bigger swings in value.

Exam trap. This statement is false: High returns can be earned without any additional risk.

Liquidity risk

Liquidity risk is the chance a fund cannot sell securities quickly without a price discount, such as in thinly traded bonds.

Example. A fund finds it hard to sell a bond that few people trade, except at a lower price.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Liquidity risk affects only equity funds.
