Key terms, examples and exam traps

Bond

A bond is a debt instrument paying periodic interest (coupon) and repaying principal at maturity.

Example. An investor lends to a company and receives coupons.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A bond gives ownership in the company.

Coupon and yield

The coupon is the fixed interest on face value, while yield is the return based on current price; yield rises when price falls.

Example. A bond bought below face value yields more than its coupon.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Yield and price move in the same direction.

Duration

Duration measures a bond's price sensitivity to interest-rate changes; higher duration means larger price swings.

Example. A long bond falls more than a short one when rates rise.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Duration is the bond's maturity date.

Credit risk

Credit risk is the possibility that the issuer fails to pay interest or principal on time.

Example. A bond rated BBB is more exposed than one rated AAA.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Government bonds carry the highest credit risk.

Credit rating

A credit rating is an agency's opinion of an issuer's ability to repay, from AAA (highest) downward.

Example. A bond is rated AA+ by a rating agency.

Exam trap. This statement is false: A credit rating is a guarantee of repayment.

Government security

A G-sec is issued by the government with negligible credit risk, though price changes with yields.

Example. A manager holds G-secs for safety and liquidity.

Exam trap. This statement is false: G-secs have no price risk.

Money market instruments

Money market instruments mature within a year and are used to park short-term funds.

Example. A portfolio holds T-bills for liquidity.

Exam trap. This statement is false: Money market instruments mature in 10 years.
